Want to show customers you care? Look to “the researcher”

The key to customer engagement is to create a website that is trustworthy, clear, and supports the customer’s goals.

On this page

    In the previous article we broke customer engagement into “Essential Engagement” (basic manners) and “Targeted Engagement” (marketing). In this article we’ll take a deeper dive into Essential Engagement using the persona of “The Researcher” to get a better understanding of the customer’s perspective.

    “A persona is a fictional, yet realistic, description of a typical or target user of the product. Personas help product teams empathize with users.”1

    — Taylor Dykes (NNGroup)

    Using the Researcher persona we can remove a significant amount of guesswork from customer engagement. Understanding how customers want to engage with your business helps you establish a framework for creating successful customer engagement.

    Who is “The Researcher”?

    “The Researcher” was created by the Nielsen Norman Group, described in the article “Designing for 5 Types of E-Commerce Shoppers”2 written by Amy Schade. The “5 Types of Shoppers” describes the wide-range of e-commerce customer personalities, detailing their motivations and pain-points. These 5 personas can help create “well-rounded” websites that support a spectrum of customer motivations and needs.

    While the specific goals of the e-commerce shoppers may be different from your customers’, I strongly believe the motivations translate to almost any industry.

    The 5 Types of Shoppers

    ShopperGoalBehaviour
    Product FocusedSpeed - Find the product and check-out as quickly as possible.They know the specific product they want to purchase, and may have purchased it before.
    BrowsersLeisure shopping - They enjoy looking at websites to pass the time or for inspiration.These customers enjoy seeing how information on a website has changed between visits (looking at new products, current sales, etc.).
    ResearchersFind the perfect product - A product that will suit their goals, wants, or needs.They collect info on products and prices across numerous businesses.
    Bargain HuntersBest possible deal.They work quickly, scanning for key product and business information (prices, shipping, etc.).
    One-Time ShoppersBuy something and leave.They’re visiting your website due to a one-time need (e.g., to use a gift card).

    They’re completely unfamiliar with your products and website, and need clear, consistent navigation.

    Why is the Researcher Key to Essential Engagement?

    #1 — The benchmarks of the Researcher and Essential Engagement are indistinguishable

    Essential Engagement BenchmarksResearcher Motivations
    Support each step of the customer process (research, purchasing, customer support), making it as seamless as possible.Researchers have the highest needs for support, requiring the widest variety of information about your products and services.
    Clear and efficient communication of business (FAQ, return policy) and product (prices, sizing, etc.) information.Researchers are more likely to notice the efficiency of your website as they search through numerous sites for the same pieces of information.
    Trustworthy as demonstrated by a business’s transparency around exchanges between the customer and the business (clear return policy, shipping policy, well designed FAQ).Researchers are looking for quality products and a business that won’t waste their time.

    #2 — Let’s keep it simple

    For people new to Engagement and UX Design, I believe it’s better to start by engaging with one customer base (e.g., the Researcher) on a single platform (e.g., your website). By starting simple, it’s easier to see when you’ve “hit the mark”, rather than trying to aimlessly engage with swaths of customers you don’t yet understand.

    #3 — Researchers are sleuths

    The Researcher’s perspective can help find major gaps in website engagement. Researchers search for information on web-pages that often get neglected. Their perspective can work to help businesses realize the full-potential of their website like footers, menus, FAQ, reviews, etc., making essential web-elements work for the customer, rather than implementing engagement band-aids (e.g., chat-bots, pop-ups, etc.). These engagement band-aids provide minimal customer support, after the customer has already felt frustrated by poor website design.

    #4 — The Researcher’s motivations prevent hollow engagement

    The Researcher’s motivations prevent us from implementing engagement methods that are hollow and aimless. Because at the end of the day:

    How can we create customer loyalty without Trust?

    How can we engage with customers without Clarity?

    How can we deliver solid user experience without supporting Customer Goals?

    What are the Researcher’s Motivations?

    Enjoy Completing the Goal, not the Process

    Unlike customers who enjoy browsing through sites and products, the process of shopping isn’t what brings the Researcher joy (positive reinforcement). The Researcher feels joy at the end of the purchasing process, when they:

    1. Observe that the purchasing process has been relatively painless.
    2. Feel that they’ve chosen the product that meets their goals (the purchase has been paid for).
    3. Perceive the product is high-quality (the product is in their hands).

    I’m searching for the perfect commuter car for me and my family. It needs to match my budget and keep my family as safe as possible. Can you help me?

    Evaluating Trust in your Business

    When Researchers trawl through numerous websites to gather information, they’re also searching for signs that a business is trustworthy. These customers want evidence that a business respects their time and effort.

    Researchers have a specific purpose for their ideal product; they’re searching for something that can help them meet a goal, need, hope, etc. Some customers may “shoot for the moon”, but all customers are putting trust in the website to give them an accurate description of its products, as well as the business’ services (price, product details, shipping, returns, etc.).

    I’m really tall, so it’s hard to buy jeans that fit. It means a lot when a site provides detailed product measurements and is transparent about their return policy.

    Reducing Effort through Clarity

    Since Researchers don’t enjoy the shopping experience, businesses can make the customer’s process of finding their ideal product faster and easier by clearly displaying information and having seamless site navigation (menus). “If your site offers limited or unclear product information, [Researchers] won’t spend their time researching, let alone buying, on a suboptimal site”2.

    After searching through 4 pages I couldn’t find any information on their return policy. I gave up and decided to look at a better business.

    Personality: Planners

    Researchers often visit multiple websites in order to gather information and compare their options. The process differs for each customer; some may quickly compare a number of products found on one website before purchasing (Do I like the red or the green shirt?). Other customers may collect detailed information over multiple research sessions, in order to better understand the array of available products and businesses within a specific market.

    Over the last couple of weeks I’ve been researching all the possible features of commuter cars. Specifically, I’m comparing all these cars based on their safety rating and gas mileage.

    Why are some People “Researchers”?

    People in Flux: Making Changes & Covid19

    “During Covid 76% of consumers have changed stores, brands, or the way they shop.”3

    For a period, Covid turned everyone into a Researcher:

    • Researching Covid-fighting products and practices (masks, sanitizers, hand washing, distancing, etc.).
    • Searching to find products and services online that they would normally buy in-store (groceries, buying specific holiday gifts, etc.).
    • Navigating the additional complexities of shopping online (shipping costs, border taxes, shipping times, errors, etc.).

    People were looking to fulfill their needs in the most effective and safe ways possible, which involves sustained effort and research. By understanding and designing for the Researcher’s perspective, businesses can work towards supporting stressed customers. And when customers are less stressed, your customer-support team is less stressed.

    People who are “Othered” by the Commerce Landscape

    Some people are faced with a commerce landscape that inadequately fulfills their needs, forcing them to be diligent researchers.

    People who are fat.
    People of colour.
    People with disabilities.
    People with chronic illnesses.

    Some people have few options, while others can barely find products that meet their basic needs. Either-way, shopping can force people to be aware of how society perceives them as “other”.

    Websites that accommodate Researchers’ motivations (planning, trust, clarity) can help to offset the extra effort (emotional, time, money) that many people are forced to take-on.

    What are the Researcher’s Pain-Points?

    The Researcher has explicit (clear) and implicit (indirect) pain-points. Explicit pain-points prevent the Researcher from achieving what motivates them:

    • Experiences that distract customers from their goal or impede their ability to plan.
      E.g., pop-ups, poor website navigation, ineffective menus.
    • Content and navigation that are vague or confusing to the user.
      E.g., vague information on shipping prices and process.
    • Interactions that create mistrust in the customer.
      E.g., websites that lack shipping information, appropriate size descriptions, etc.

    Then there are the two implicit pain-points of the Researcher: Cognitive Overload and Choice Overload. Combating these implicit pain-points helps us to create websites that understand customers better than they might understand themselves.

    Cognitive Overload

    There are limits to how much information a person can process; “when the amount of information coming in exceeds our ability to handle it… performance suffers.”4 We’ve all felt the effects of Cognitive Overload:

    • You’re trying to keep up with a conversation, and you forget what you were just about to say.
    • Someone asks you a question, and you have to ask them to repeat it… three times.
    • You’ve had a terrible day, and someone’s awful driving is making you feel way more angry than it would on an average day.
    When we’re cognitively overloaded, we get overwhelmed, and we make mistakes.

    If the customer continues deeper into this cognitive overload loop, the effects of cognitive overload get worse, and the customer gets more frustrated. The effects of this feedback-loop depend on the customer’s ability to deal with frustration. Often, when customers reach peak frustration they “… get overwhelmed and abandon the task.”4

    So, what is the cognitive load of an E-Commerce website?

    1. The effort to absorb & search for new information.
      Learning about new products, searching for shipping info, etc.
    2. Tracking goals and constraints.
      How many of x product do I want to buy? In what colours? How much money can I spend today? Will it get here on time for Christmas?
    3. Comparing information between different products and/or sites.
      Comparing prices or shipping tax between website x and y, etc.
    4. The effort of navigating the website.
      Understanding a specific site’s menu UI in order to get to the sale section, etc.
    5. Navigating ineffective design-decisions and distractions.
      Having to search through long paragraphs for one small piece of info, dealing with pop-ups, ignoring distracting banner ads, etc.

    This is where things get difficult for Researchers (and many other ecommerce customers). The Cognitive Load listed above is manageable for one website, but Researchers are multitaskers. The more websites they visit, the more information they take on, and they quickly become cognitively overloaded.

    “51% of consumers shop online while also performing tasks competing for their attention.”5

    We can use George A. Miller’s “Working Memory”6 theory to estimate how much information a customer can hold in their mind before becoming overwhelmed. Generally, people can hold 7 pieces of information (+/- 2)6. The cognitive load of an E-Commerce site (seen in the list above) is at least 5 pieces of information. Recognizing that Working Memory is a heuristic (meaning it’s a guideline, based on study, not the absolute truth for all situations and all customers), it’s likely the average ecommerce customer would become cognitively overloaded after researching two websites.

    It may seem like a paradox: the more informed the Researcher, the more difficult it is for them to effectively achieve their goal. This helps to explain why clarity and trust are so important to Researchers. If website content is clear (menus, FAQ, product descriptions, etc.), the customer needs to spend less energy trawling through pages for specific information. A clear website allows customers to focus on making an informed decision, rather than unnecessarily overwhelming them.

    Choice Overload

    It can feel like there’s an overwhelming amount of options online. When customers are no longer limited by what stores are found around their home, their choices increase exponentially7.

    “As the number of choices increases, so does the effort required to collect information and make good decisions.”7

    Researchers can be enticed by the possibility of finding their “best possible product”, becoming cognitively overloaded by the amount of work necessary to sort through a multitude of stores and products.

    There’s no question, our Working Memory can only handle so much information. With this knowledge, when designing your website it’s best to assume ecommerce customers are feeling Cognitively Overloaded.
